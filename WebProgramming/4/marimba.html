Percussion / Marimba

Marimba

The marimba is a variety of xylophone, and is one of the many African-derived percussion instruments that developed in the Americas. It is a pitched percussion instrument with wooden bars arranged like a piano keyboard, with resonators below each bar. The instrument is known for its warm, rich tone and is used in a variety of musical genres, from traditional folk music to contemporary classical compositions.

How it makes sound

Players strike the bars with mallets wrapped in yarn, cord, or rubber. Harder mallets produce a strong, clear sound, while softer mallets emphasize the marimba's warm and rounded tone. The bars are suspended so they can vibrate freely, and the resonators beneath them amplify the fundamental pitch and its overtones.

The marimba's bars are arranged in two rows, with the natural notes on the lower row and the accidentals on the upper row. Players can use two, four, or more mallets to play chords and complex passages.

Some techniques involve rolling the mallets across the bars to create a sustained sound, while others use rapid alternation to produce fast passages. Strokes can be "muted" by pressing on the bar after striking it, or "open" by allowing the bar to vibrate freely. The combination of mallet choice, striking technique, and bar spacing gives the marimba its distinctive voice.

The marimba can often be played by multiple performers, especially with larger, concert-sized instruments. In these cases, players may divide the keyboard range among themselves, allowing for complex arrangements and harmonies.

History and development

The marimba has roots in African musical traditions, where similar instruments were made from wooden bars and gourds. It was brought to the Americas by enslaved Africans, and it evolved in Central America, particularly in Guatemala and Mexico. In these regions, the marimba became an important part of local music and culture, while also being adapted for concert performance. Here, the wooden bars were supported by a frame, or were hung at the player's waist.

Marimba traditions in Central America reflect the meeting of African, Indigenous, and European musical histories. In Guatemala, the marimba is closely connected with national and regional identity, and ensembles perform both community music and concert performance. The instrument should therefore be understood as more than a large wooden version of a xylophone.

The modern concert marimba arose in the United States in the early 20th century. Here, it became a tube-resonated instrument that sits at a lower octave than the xylophone.The resonators are graduated in length: longer tubes support lower notes and shorter tubes support higher notes. Some instruments include adjustable resonators to compensate for changes in room temperature and humidity.

Marimba music can combine melody, harmony, and rhythm. A single player may use two, four, or more mallets, changing grip and rebound to play chords and fast lines. The sound depends as much on the player's touch and spacing as on the instrument's pitch.

Marimba builders and performers continue to expand the instrument's breadth. Arrangements may feature regional dance styles, classical works, contemporary solo music, and chamber pieces. Its wide dynamic range lets a performer move from a bell-like melody to a dense, resonant chord within one phrase.

Listening details

  • Bars: Tuned wooden keys provide the pitched surface that the player strikes.
  • Resonators: Tubes below the bars reinforce the sound and extend its sustain.
  • Mallets: Their hardness changes the balance between attack and warmth.
