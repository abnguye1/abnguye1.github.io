Percussion / Piano

Piano

The piano is a keyboard instrument that turns a player's finger movement into the vibration of strings. Felt-covered hammers strike the strings, and a soundboard amplifies them. It belongs in the percussion branch of this explorer because its strings are set in motion by hammers rather than directly plucked.

How it makes sound

Pressing a key activates a carefully balanced action. The key moves a hammer toward one or more strings, then the hammer returns instantly so the string can continue vibrating. A damper normally stops the string when the key is released, allowing for precise articulation.

A piano combines a keyboard, action, strings, bridges, a frame, and a soundboard. Lower notes use longer and thicker strings, while higher notes use shorter strings. Many notes use two or three strings tuned together to increase volume and richness. The cast iron frame of a modern piano supports the massive tension created by its strings.

A pianist can alter volume and tone profoundly through touch and attack speed. The pedals provide additional control on most concert pianos. The right pedal lifts all dampers at once, allowing sympathetic resonance and sustain. The una corda (soft) and sostenuto pedals offer further variation by shifting the action or sustaining only specific notes.

History and development

Bartolomeo Cristofori developed the early piano in Italy around the beginning of the eighteenth century. Its most important new idea was the ability to respond differently to soft and forceful playing. That dynamic control distinguished it from earlier keyboard instruments, like the harpsichord, whose volume was less directly shaped by the player's touch.

Over the next century, the instrument underwent significant design changes to accommodate demands for greater volume and a wider range. Improvements in steel strings, iron frames, and double-escapement action design helped the piano project over large orchestras and fill grand concert halls.

These manufacturing advancements also helped the piano become common in homes, schools, churches, and studios. It became an essential tool for composition and education, capable of expressing both complex harmonies and sweeping melodies simultaneously.

Composers and performers quickly made it central to classical music, and its influence only grew over time. Today, the piano remains a foundational voice across ragtime, jazz, popular songwriting, film music, and digital music production.

Listening details

  • Attack: The hammer's speed changes the energy and character of the beginning of a note.
  • Resonance: The soundboard and undamped strings allow neighboring notes to color one another.
  • Pedal: Sustaining a note changes the space between sounds as much as it changes their length.
