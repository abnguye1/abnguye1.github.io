Percussion / Snare drum

Snare drum

The snare drum is a shallow, two-headed drum known for its sharp attack and buzzing response. It's present in orchestras, marching bands, drum sets, military music, and many other musical contexts.

How it makes sound

A shell supports a batter head on top and a resonant head below. A set of metal wires, called snares, rests against the underside of the bottom head. When the top head vibrates, the snare wires respond with a dry, textured buzz. Somewhere on the shell, is a switch called a throw-off that can lift the snares away from the head when a player wants a more "open" drum sound.

The shell may be made from wood, metal, or a composite material. Hoops and tension rods control the tightness of the heads, while the size and depth of the shell affect the drum's response. A deep shell produces a deeper, more resonant sound, while a shallow shell gives a crisp, cutting sound.

Players can strike the head with sticks, brushes, or mallets. The head's tension and the snares' tension can be adjusted to change the drum's response.

Sound can also be shaped by the player's technique, including where and how the head is struck, the angle of attack, and the use of rolls or accents. Rimshots, where the stick strikes both the head and the rim simultaneously, produce a louder, sharper noise, while cross-sticking, where the stick rests on the head and strikes the rim, creates a softer, more muted sound.

Key to the performance of the snare drum are rudiments, which are standard patterns of strokes that form the basis of snare drum technique. These include rolls, flams, drags, and paradiddles, among others. Mastery of these rudiments allows drummers to execute complex rhythms and textures with precision and musicality.

History and development

Before the snare drum, snares originated in ancient Egypt, which is assumed to have been placed on instruments like the tambourine. The snare drum as we know it today developed from European tabor drums used in military and civic music. Originaly, snares were placed on the upper head of the tabor drum, or sometimes on both heads. Larger versions of the tabor drum became the foundation for the snare drum, when two sticks began to be used, with snares being placed on the bottom head.

At this point, they tended to be suspended with a strap or rope, placed on the players left side (due to the rifle being on the right side), and spread across Europe. This left-sided placement is still common, and is the reason for the "traditional" grip, where the left hand holds the stick differently than the right hand.

Drummers used repeating patterns to support marching and to communicate signals. Over time, the instrument moved into concert bands and orchestras, where composers used it for a trademark military noise, suspense, dance rhythms, and precise articulation.

In the twentieth century, the snare became one of the central voices of the drum set. Jazz, rock, funk, marching percussion, film scoring, and studio production each developed different approaches to its tuning and touch.

Materials used to make snare drums have changed over time. Early snares were made from gut, horsehair, or metal wire. In the 19th century, drums became shallow, with a brass shell and rods. The throw-off mechanism was developed in the 20th century, allowing players to lift the snares away from the head.

Listening details

  • Buzz: The snares create the dry rattle that separates this drum from a tom or bass drum.
  • Accent: A rimshot produces a louder, sharper noise by striking the head and rim together.
  • Roll: Alternating strokes create a sustained texture instead of a single isolated hit.
