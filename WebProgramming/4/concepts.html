There's a lot to music.

Music brings together sound, art, memories, and people. These notes explain concepts about music that you can use to listen, analyze, and understand instruments and music itself.

On Instruments

What are the main ways instruments are grouped?

Hornbostel-Sachs organizes instruments by the material that vibrates: idiophones, membranophones, chordophones, aerophones, and electrophones. An idiophone makes sound through the vibration of its own body, a membranophone uses a stretched membrane, a chordophone uses strings, and an aerophone uses a vibrating column of air. Electrophones depend on electrical circuits or amplification. This system sits alongside the family names used by orchestras and helps explain why very different instruments can share similar musical roles. For example, a violin and guitar are both chordophones because strings vibrate, while a snare drum is a membranophone because its stretched head vibrates. The categories describe the sound source, but not the way the instrument is played or its size.

How do materials affect sound?

Wood, skin, metal, bamboo, and synthetic materials each carry different manufacturing traditions. A change in available material can change an instrument's volume, tuning, or social role. Spruce and maple give a violin a light, resonant body, while a steel guitar string produces a different attack from a gut or nylon string. Drum skin responds to thickness and tension, and bamboo can give a flute a soft, flexible tone. Brass instruments are shaped mostly by the length and shape of their tubing, mouthpiece, and bell, but the metal's stiffness and construction still affect durability and response.

Musical building blocks

What is timbre?

Sound comes from vibration, but timbre is what lets us tell two sounds apart (like a trumpet vs. a flute) even when they play the same pitch. The combination of frequencies, attack, sustain, and decay gives each instrument its own sound. Additional noises (like breath, string buzz, or reed rattle) and the way a player shapes the sound can also affect timbre.

How do pitch and melody work together?

Pitch describes how fast a sound vibrates, while melody organizes pitches into a memorable line shaped by direction, repetition, dynamics, and space. Additionally, the way and context in which a melody is played can influence its perception. Scales and modes provide the pitch materials from which many melodies draw.

How does music organize time?

Music organizes time through rhythm, tempo, meter, and syncopation. Rhythm is how sounds are organized over time, typically with a certain pattern. Tempo is the specific speed at which the rhythm is played (usually in BPM). Meter groups beats into recurring patterns of emphasis (like a strong-weak pattern), while syncopation is used to break up predictable patterns (like placing accents on off-beats). A groove is a repeated rhythmic feel that can create a sense of movement or drive in the music.

What is harmony?

Harmony describes how pitches sound together to create a specific sound. A parallel harmony uses the same interval between notes, while a counterpoint harmony uses independent lines that interact. Certain harmonies can create tension, while others feel stable or resolved. One typical harmony is a triad, which combines three pitches that are a third apart (such as C-E-G).

Music and culture

Music can accompany a ritual, tell a story, support movement, mark identity, or create a shared social experience. The same instrument may have different meanings in different communities. A drum can be a concert instrument, a signal, a dance partner, or part of a sacred ceremony depending on who plays it and why.

Instruments change when they travel. Builders adapt imported designs to local materials, climate, tuning systems, and musical priorities. Performers make new styles by combining older practices with new technologies, audiences, and places. Keyboard instruments, for example, were adapted to different tuning systems in Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. The guitar traveled from Spain to the Americas, where it became central to many musical traditions.

Notation can preserve pitch, rhythm, dynamics, and structure, but not every detail of a performance. Oral traditions preserve music through listening, imitation, repeated practice, and relationships between teachers and students. West African griots, or jeliw, have transmitted the Sundiata epic, family histories, and musical patterns through generations. In Japan, noh and shamisen repertories have also been taught through performance lineages, where students learn style and timing by listening closely to a teacher rather than reading every detail from a score.
