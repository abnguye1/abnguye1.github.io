Woodwinds / Bassoon

Bassoon

The bassoon is a large double-reed woodwind instrument renowned for its distinctive, folded wooden body and its versatile voice. It can anchor an ensemble with warm, serious low notes, yet its quick articulation and unusual upper register make it highly effective for lyrical solos and characterful, even humorous, musical passages.

How it makes sound

Sound begins when the player blows through a double reed—two pieces of cane tied together—which is attached to a curved metal tube called a bocal. The vibrating reeds set the air column in motion inside the instrument's conical bore.

Because the bassoon's wooden tube is nearly nine feet long, it is "doubled" or folded in half. This allows the air to travel down the boot joint and back up through the bell, making the instrument compact enough to hold. A complex system of metal keys and tone holes spans this long body, enabling the performer to change pitches across a wide range.

The player's embouchure, air pressure, and voicing heavily dictate the instrument's tone. A looser embouchure and slower air might produce a dark, rumbling low note, while a firmer bite and faster air can coax out a focused, nasal, and surprisingly bright upper register.

History and development

The modern bassoon evolved from earlier Renaissance double-reed instruments, most notably the dulcian, which was carved from a single piece of wood. By the seventeenth century, makers in France began constructing the instrument in separate joints, which improved its intonation and allowed for a more consistent internal bore.

Over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the bassoon underwent significant mechanical refinements. German maker Wilhelm Heckel developed a key system and bore design that became the standard for the modern instrument, ensuring it could project reliably and meet the expanding technical demands of orchestral composers.

In the orchestra, bassoons frequently reinforce the low strings and brass, providing a rich bass foundation. However, composers also rely on them for expressive mid-register melodies and rapid, staccato counterpoint.

Today, the bassoon is a staple in orchestras, concert bands, and chamber ensembles. Its larger sibling, the contrabassoon, extends the family's role even lower, supplying a deep, resonant rumble that grounds the entire woodwind section.

Listening details

  • Timbre: Listen for the contrast between its dark, rounded bottom octave and the more pinched, nasal quality of its high notes.
  • Articulation: The double reed allows for incredibly crisp, rapid staccato notes, often used by composers for nimble or comic effect.
  • Blend: In an ensemble, its warm tone can seamlessly merge with cellos or horns, adding subtle depth without overpowering other voices.
