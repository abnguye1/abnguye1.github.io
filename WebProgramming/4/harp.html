Strings / Harp

Harp

The harp is an ancient and mesmerizing chordophone featuring an open triangular frame of strings that rise directly from a resonant wooden soundboard. Celebrated for its crystalline tone, layered polyphonic textures, and sweeping cascades of color, the harp allows a performer's fingertips to pluck open strings directly without a bow, plectrum, or hammer, creating an intimate, physical bond between touch and sound.

How it makes sound

Sound is generated when the player plucks strings directly with the pads of the thumbs and first three fingers. Releasing a plucked string allows it to vibrate along its full unstopped length, producing an immediate, clear attack followed by a warm, ringing acoustic decay. The little finger is not used due to its shorter reach and weaker muscular control.

The modern concert grand harp is structured around a rigid triangular frame: a vertical pillar (column) that withstands over two thousand pounds of string tension, a curved neck containing complex mechanical linkages, and a hollow wooden soundbox (body). The strings anchor into a tapered spruce soundboard that is thick at the base for deep bass resonance and thin at the top to support delicate, high-register clarity.

Unlike fretted or bowed instruments where fingers press strings against a neck to change pitch, each harp string is dedicated to a single diatonic note. Pitch is altered on concert instruments using seven foot pedals located around the base (one for each pitch letter: D, C, B, E, F, G, A). Each pedal controls a three-position mechanical rod system running up through the column to rotating brass discs (fourchettes) on the neck, raising or lowering all octaves of that note by a half step (flat, natural, sharp) simultaneously.

Harpists shape timbre through varied hand positions and contact points. Plucking low near the soundboard (près de la table) creates a dry, nasal, guitar-like sharpness, while plucking in the middle of the string yields a rounded, resonant warmth. Performers also execute bell-like harmonics by lightly touching the midpoint of a string with the side of the palm while plucking with the thumb, or damp vibrations with flat hands to control phrasing and silence.

Because the pedals can preset strings to any enharmonically spelled chord or scale, the performer can sweep their hands across multiple octaves in a single continuous gesture. This produces the iconic glissando—a shimmering wave of sound that can range from an airy whisper to a dramatic, room-filling orchestral swell.

History and development

Harps rank among humanity's earliest documented musical instruments, dating back over five thousand years. Early arched or bow harps evolved from the archer's hunting bow, depicted in ancient Sumerian wall carvings, Babylonian cylinder seals, and Egyptian tomb frescoes. In ancient Greece and the Mediterranean, frame harps and related lyres were revered for philosophical contemplation, epic poetry recitation, and spiritual ritual.

In medieval Europe, portable frame harps flourished in the courts of troubadours and minstrel poets. In Ireland and the Scottish Highlands, the wire-strung Celtic harp (clàrsach) held immense cultural, spiritual, and political significance. Accompanied by hereditary bards who preserved history and family lineage, these instruments were carved from single willow logs and played with long fingernails.

As European Renaissance and Baroque music embraced complex chromatic modulations, diatonic harps struggled to keep up with orchestral demands. Makers experimented with crossed double-strung harps and manual hand hooks. Around 1720, Bavarian craftsman Jacob Hochbrucker invented the first single-action pedal harp, using foot pedals to raise pitches by a half step while leaving both hands free to pluck.

The crowning breakthrough occurred in 1810, when French instrument maker Sébastien Érard patented the double-action pedal harp. By equipping each pedal with two rotating discs, Érard allowed players to play in all twenty-four major and minor keys. This transformed the harp into a fully chromatic concert instrument, inspiring 19th- and 20th-century composers such as Hector Berlioz, Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky, Claude Debussy, and Maurice Ravel to weave its radiant colors into the symphonic canon.

Contemporary concert grand harps typically carry forty-seven strings made of nylon, sheep gut, and wire-wound steel. Alongside orchestral concert grands, non-pedal lever harps continue to thrive in Celtic, folk, and early music, while modern electric and MIDI harps expand the instrument into jazz, pop, and electronic experimental soundscapes.

Listening details

  • Glissando: A sweeping, fluid stroke across adjacent strings that produces a shimmering, cascading wave of harmony.
  • Harmonics: Crystalline, bell-like tones produced by lightly stopping the midpoint of a string with the palm while plucking.
  • Près de la table: Plucking close to the soundboard to generate a tight, dry, and nasal acoustic timbre.
